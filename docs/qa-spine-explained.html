Product briefing · August 2026 · For non-technical readers

qa-spine, explained

What we built, what it promises, how a customer starts using it, and how to prepare a demo — written so you don't need to be an engineer to follow any of it.

The problem we solve

Software teams everywhere now use AI to do quality work: the AI reads a feature request, writes the checks, and flags problems. That saves enormous time — and creates a new problem nobody had five years ago:

When something goes wrong, can you prove what the AI did, what it was told, and whether any human ever looked at its work?

Today, for most teams, the honest answer is no. The AI's work is scattered across chat windows and files. Nothing connects "what we asked for" to "what we checked" to "why we decided to release." And regulators, auditors, and customers are starting to ask for exactly that connection.

What we built

qa-spine is a tamper-proof logbook for software quality. Like a flight recorder on an aircraft: it doesn't fly the plane, it makes an unerasable record of everything that happened — so that afterwards, anyone can reconstruct exactly what was known, and when.

Every entry in the logbook is linked to the one before it, forming a chain from the original ask all the way to the final release decision:

Link 1 The ask What the business requested, in its original words.
→
Link 2 The questions Unclear parts the AI flagged before any work began.
→
Link 3 The checks The tests written to verify the feature works.
→
Link 4 The results Whether each check passed or failed, and when.
→
Link 5 The decision Ship or hold — with the reason, on the record.

One important thing qa-spine does not do: it never does the thinking itself. Your team's AI assistant does the analysis; qa-spine checks the work and writes it into the logbook. That's a feature, not a gap — it means the product works with any AI, and none of your company's data is ever sent anywhere. Everything stays on your own machines.

Five promises it keeps

These aren't aspirations — each one is enforced by the software itself:

Nothing can be erased

The logbook only ever grows, like a bank ledger. A mistake is corrected by adding a correction — the original stays visible, along with the fact that it was corrected. Even someone with direct access to the files can't quietly rewrite history.

Every AI entry is signed

Any entry made by an AI must state which AI made it and which version of the instructions it was following. An unsigned AI entry is refused at the door.

The AI cannot misquote

When the AI flags a problem in a request, it must quote the request word for word. If the quote doesn't actually appear in the original text, the entire submission is rejected. Made-up evidence cannot enter the record.

No quiet ship

If a known question is still unanswered, the system refuses to record a "ship" decision — unless a person explicitly overrides it with a written reason. That override is stamped on the record permanently, and it's the first thing anyone reviewing the release will see.

Plain language, enforced

The reason attached to a release decision must be readable by a project manager. If it's written in internal engineering shorthand, the system asks for a rewrite. The release summary anyone receives is guaranteed readable.

The story you show in a demo

The product ships with a ready-made example — a small story, and a persuasive one. Here's what the audience sees:

The ask arrives

A feature request comes in: "Refunds must be processed quickly and the customer notified appropriately." It sounds fine. It isn't.

The AI raises its hand

Before anything is built, the AI flags two problems. "Quickly" — how quick is quick? Nobody can test a feeling. And "notified appropriately" — what happens if the notification fails to send? The request doesn't say.

One question gets answered

The team defines "quickly" as within 3 business days and writes a check for it. That question is settled, on the record.

One question gets ignored

The notification-failure question is never answered. Weeks later, the refund check fails in exactly that way: "timed out waiting for notification." The ignored question predicted the failure.

Release day

The verdict: HOLD  — and the record shows precisely why: a known ambiguity, flagged on day one, never resolved, now failing. Who knew what, and when, is one click away.

That's the whole pitch in ninety seconds: a vague sentence became a real failure, and the logbook caught it at the gate — with receipts.

Running it (three lines, copy and paste)

Open a terminal in the project folder and paste these. The first prepares the software, the second writes the example story into a fresh demo logbook, the third opens the viewer in your browser:

Terminal — start the demo
npm run build
QA_SPINE_DB=demo/trace.db node examples/seed-demo.ts
QA_SPINE_DB=demo/trace.db npm run inspect   # then open http://127.0.0.1:4700

In the viewer, click Chain to see the story left-to-right (the unanswered question glows red), click any entry to see the AI's quote highlighted inside the original request, and use the Queue view to answer the open question live — the red clears in front of the audience. Finish by generating the one-page release summary (copy the decision's ID from the list view):

Terminal — the executive summary page
QA_SPINE_DB=demo/trace.db node dist/server.js brief <decision-id> > brief.html

That produces a single, self-contained web page saying HOLD and why — the thing you'd actually email to a project head. End the demo on it.

How a customer comes on board

The journey is deliberately small. There is no account to create, no data to upload to us, no AI subscription to buy from us:

  1. Install it on their machine

    Today: a five-minute setup by one engineer (it needs only Node.js, a standard free tool). On the near-term roadmap: a single command, npx qa-spine, with no setup at all.

  2. Connect their AI assistant

    One line tells the customer's existing AI assistant (Claude, or any compatible tool) that the logbook exists. From then on, the assistant records its QA work there automatically.

  3. Load their real requirements

    An included importer pulls actual tickets from Jira, the tracking tool most teams already use — so within the first hour they see their own feature requests being questioned, not our examples.

  4. The team answers questions

    The viewer shows every unanswered question the AI raised. People answer them; each answer permanently retires its question. An empty queue is the goal.

  5. Release day changes

    Before shipping, they ask the logbook: "anything still open on what this release touches?" The one-page summary — ship or hold, and why — goes to whoever signs off. That page is the habit that makes the product sticky.

Need different demo data?

The built-in refund story fits most audiences, but if you're demoing to, say, a bank, a healthcare company, or your own team, a story in their world lands harder. Three ways to get one, easiest first:

  1. Ask the AI assistant to write you a new story

    The product is built to be driven by an AI assistant — so use that. Tell Claude (in this project): "Copy examples/seed-demo.ts to examples/seed-banking.ts and rewrite the story for a loan-approval feature — keep the same structure." Then run it exactly like the original, pointing at a fresh file. This is the recommended route for a non-engineer.

  2. Edit the story file yourself

    The file examples/seed-demo.ts contains the story as ordinary English sentences inside quotation marks — the request, the two problems, the failure message, the verdict reason. Change only the words inside the quotes. One rule to respect: the short quoted phrases (like "quickly") must appear word-for-word inside your rewritten request sentence — that's the honesty check doing its job, and it applies to you too.

  3. Use the customer's real tickets

    For the strongest demo of all, the Jira importer pulls their actual requirements, and the AI audits those live. Nothing is more convincing than the product finding a genuinely vague sentence in the prospect's own backlog.

One habit to keep: always point demos at a fresh file (that's what QA_SPINE_DB=demo/trace.db does in every command above). It keeps demo material out of the real logbook — and since the logbook is deliberately unerasable, you can't tidy it up afterwards.

Honest questions, honest answers

Does this replace our QA people?

No — it makes their judgment visible and durable. The AI does the tedious volume (reading every requirement, flagging every vagueness); people decide what's acceptable. The logbook records both, and keeps them distinguishable forever.

Does it send our data to an AI company?

Never. qa-spine itself contacts no AI and no internet service. Your own AI assistant — whichever one you already use and trust — does the reading, under your existing agreement with that vendor. qa-spine only verifies and records the results, locally.

Can a clever AI cheat the system?

It can't forge evidence (misquotes are rejected) and it can't ship quietly (overrides are stamped on the record). What it could do is answer its own questions — and we deliberately allow that, because the record always shows an AI answered, in a different color, signed. The product's philosophy is visibility rather than locks: nothing dishonest can happen invisibly. We say this to customers up front; it builds trust rather than costing it.

Why would a customer pay for this?

Two reasons. Day to day: fewer failures that trace back to a vague sentence nobody questioned. On audit day: regulators and enterprise buyers increasingly require proof of exactly this — which AI produced what, from which requirement, and which human approved it. That proof is expensive to reconstruct after the fact and nearly free to record as you go. qa-spine is the recorder.

What does it cost to run?

Almost nothing. No servers, no database licenses, no per-seat AI fees from us — it runs on the team's own machines and rides on the AI subscription they already have.
